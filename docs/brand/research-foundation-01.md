# Vaani Identity — Research Foundation 01

**Status:** Research foundation only · not a logo proposal · not approved artwork  
**Scope:** Cultural and product context, visual principles, research-backed design territories, and gates for the first genuine lettering studies.

---

## 1. The problem we are actually solving

Vaani needs an identity that can be recognized without relying on a familiar devotional ornament, a generic sound symbol, or a decorative calligraphy font. The wordmark and its square derivative must feel like two expressions of one authored visual idea.

The identity must work in monochrome before color is considered. It must be culturally specific without pretending that one historical script, manuscript style, or religious motif represents the whole tradition. It must also belong to a contemporary product: clear in a browser tab, composed in a reading interface, and distinctive on a printed invitation or publication.

**The central design question:** What visual form can express the relationship between a poetic text, its voiced transmission, and the people who keep it alive—without illustrating those ideas literally?

## 2. What the research establishes

### 2.1 Vaani exists across text, voice, and transmission

Richard David Williams's study of eighteenth-century Rādhāvallabhī networks describes song texts circulating through manuscript books and social gatherings, particularly samāj gāyan. The article makes a useful distinction for this project: a song text is not isolated from its repeated performances and hearings. Its meaning accumulates through transmission, memory, and shared experience.

This gives the identity a more grounded conceptual basis than “sound”, “music”, or “spirituality” alone. Vaani is connected to a body of poetic material and to the practices through which that material continues to be encountered.

**Design implication:** Do not default to a waveform, speaker, musical note, or abstract sound ring. Those symbols describe audio technology, not the particular relationship between poetic material and its transmission.

Source: Richard David Williams, “Dreams, songs and letters: Sectarian networks and musical archives in eighteenth-century North India,” *The Indian Economic & Social History Review* 57, no. 4 (2020), 583–604. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/0019464620948723  
Accessible author manuscript: https://eprints.soas.ac.uk/34195/7/0019464620948723.pdf

### 2.2 Manuscripts are material objects, not generic “ancient texture”

The National Museum of India describes a manuscript collection spanning many centuries, materials, languages, and scripts, including Hindi's regional literary forms such as Braj. Its collection records also demonstrate that manuscript pages vary in format, writing, ruling, ornament, and material. A Braj manuscript record in the Royal Asiatic Society archive specifically identifies handwritten Braj in Devanagari.

These sources are useful as evidence of material and typographic diversity. They are not permission to lift a script style, border, seal, or sacred mark and repurpose it as a brand ornament.

**Design implication:** Study actual writing behavior—line organization, spacing, density, rhythm, and the relationship between text and page—rather than adding distressed paper, faux manuscript grain, decorative borders, or “heritage” flourishes.

Sources:
- National Museum, New Delhi, “Manuscripts Collection”: https://nationalmuseumindia.gov.in/en/collections/index/11
- Royal Asiatic Society Archives, “Sanskrit and Braj Manuscript” (fonds SC17): https://royalasiaticarchives.org/index.php/sanskrit-and-braj-manuscript
- Vrindavan Research Institute, manuscript and Braj cultural heritage collections: https://vribharat.org/

### 2.3 A visual identity should not collapse the tradition into one aesthetic

The available archival material spans different languages, scripts, media, and periods. The scholarship on Rādhāvallabhī song networks also describes both manuscript circulation and collective musical practice. Neither supports the shortcut “devotional subject = ornate calligraphy”.

**Design implication:** Cultural grounding must come from specific, documented observations and a clear explanation of what is being translated. A visual reference is not a design rationale by itself.

## 3. Reference study: what to inspect, and what to learn

This is a research map, not a moodboard of images to copy.

| Reference | Inspect | Legitimate design question | Do not borrow |
|---|---|---|---|
| Williams, 2020 | Relationship between written song texts, circulation, performance, and memory | Can the identity express continuity and recurrence without depicting sound? | Sectarian emblems, sacred imagery, or an assumed “devotional” style |
| National Museum manuscript collection | Different page formats, scripts, materials, ruling, and text/image relationships | How do proportion, spacing, line density, and page structure create character? | A random ornate letter, gold treatment, or generic antique texture |
| Royal Asiatic Society, SC17 | A documented Braj manuscript in Devanagari; record metadata and access conditions | What is distinctive about the written line and the page's organization? | Traced letterforms or a claim that one manuscript represents all Braj writing |
| Vrindavan Research Institute | Institutional context for Braj manuscripts, folk culture, and conservation | Which further primary sources should be consulted with cultural care? | Unverified motifs or decontextualized photographs |

**Research limitation:** This first pass establishes credible directions and primary repositories. It does not yet constitute a close paleographic study of a specific Vaani manuscript. No historical letterform should be claimed as the source of the logo until the actual object, date, script, provenance, and relevant rights or permissions have been checked.

## 4. Three design territories worth testing

These are strategic hypotheses. They are not three finished logos, and none is selected.

### Territory A — The authored line

Explore a wordmark whose character comes from the behavior of one carefully controlled line: where it begins, how it changes pressure, where it pauses, and how it resolves. The result must remain legible as “Vaani” and must not resemble a ready-made calligraphy font.

- **Potential:** A distinctive, human, memorable wordmark with a visual gesture that can be abstracted.
- **Risk:** It becomes generic brush lettering, ornamental script, or a flourish-first logo.
- **Proof required:** The letterforms must be individually authored; the signature gesture must arise naturally from the word's construction.

### Territory B — The written/voiced cadence

Explore rhythm rather than a literal sound symbol: repeated and varied intervals, pauses, continuation, and return. The source is the relationship between text and shared performance, not an audio waveform.

- **Potential:** A visual system that can extend into layouts, motion, and product transitions.
- **Risk:** It becomes a generic equalizer, repeated bars, concentric rings, or a music-app icon.
- **Proof required:** The rhythm must be visible in the wordmark's anatomy and spacing, not pasted on as a separate graphic.

### Territory C — The folio and the living text

Explore the proportion and organization of a written page: margins, line measure, density, and the relationship between a text block and the space around it. This is about editorial composition, not simulating old paper.

- **Potential:** A strong bridge between the archive, reading, and a contemporary digital product.
- **Risk:** It becomes a generic book icon, page outline, frame, or institutional publishing mark.
- **Proof required:** The idea must survive outside a page-shaped container and produce a distinctive wordmark or independent abstract form.

### How the territories should be used

Do not combine all three into one overloaded symbol. First test each as a coherent visual principle. A winning identity may draw from one territory and use another only as a supporting system principle. The primary wordmark remains the source of truth; the square derivative must be derived from its actual visual DNA, not designed as an unrelated icon.

## 5. Lettering research before vector construction

The next drawing session must begin with black-and-white lettering studies, not SVG ornament.

1. Write “Vaani” repeatedly by hand at several natural speeds. Record the forms that remain clear without forcing a calligraphic style.
2. Study the specific relationships between **V–a**, **a–a**, **a–n**, and **n–i**. The repeated “a” must feel intentional, not duplicated mechanically.
3. Test whether the capital V is genuinely part of the same system as the lowercase letters. It must not look imported from another visual language.
4. Explore the i-stem and dot as structural parts of the word. No diamond or accent is justified merely because it fills empty space.
5. Record stroke entry, exit, pressure, joins, counters, terminal shapes, and the amount of contrast. Each must have a reason.
6. Compare the word at display size and at small UI sizes. A beautiful large drawing that collapses in a favicon is not a viable identity.
7. Only after a lettering direction has a convincing silhouette should its signature gesture be isolated and tested as an abstract mark.

**Font rule:** Fonts may be used to test surrounding typography and wordmark proportions, but a font-rendered word is not evidence of bespoke lettering. Any final wordmark must be converted into carefully engineered outlines after the drawing is approved.

## 6. The square derivative: a separate acceptance test

The square mark must not be a miniature wordmark, a generic “V”, or unrelated curves inside a rounded square.

It must:
- Be traceable to a specific, identifiable feature of the approved wordmark.
- Retain a recognizable silhouette at 16 × 16 px and 32 × 32 px.
- Work as a single solid shape and in reverse.
- Remain balanced without a container; a rounded-square app tile may be a later application, not a crutch.
- Be distinguishable in a field of generic monograms and audio-service symbols.
- Have a clear construction rationale that can be explained without poetic hand-waving.

If no independent mark emerges naturally from the lettering, the correct decision is to use the wordmark alone until one does. A separate symbol is not mandatory.

## 7. Color is deliberately deferred

No new palette is proposed in this phase. Color cannot rescue weak form. The first identity tests are black on white, white on black, and one-color production.

Once the form is approved, color should be developed against the existing product environment and checked for accessibility, contrast, reproduction, and consistency. Do not assume purple, saffron, gold, terracotta, or any other culturally coded color is automatically appropriate.

## 8. The 50-point acceptance standard

Every candidate must be evaluated against all 50 criteria. Critical gates are marked **GATE**; a candidate cannot advance by averaging a high score elsewhere if it fails one of these.

## 9. Required stress tests for the first real candidates

Each candidate must be shown as:
- Wordmark, black on white.
- Wordmark, white on black.
- Wordmark at large, medium, and small sizes.
- Wordmark in a realistic header and in a reading/listening screen.
- Wordmark at favicon scale, without artificially thickening it to disguise weak construction.
- Derived abstract mark alone, with and without an app-tile background.
- One-color print and a simple embroidery simulation.
- A motion test only after the static form succeeds, with reduced-motion behavior considered.

The presentation must show actual artwork at actual sizes—not labels claiming that a test has passed.

## 10. What is intentionally not being done

- No production logo, favicon, app icon, metadata, or site asset is being changed.
- No palette is being selected.
- No old logo instance is being replaced.
- No landing-page redesign is being bundled into identity work.
- No historical script is being traced or treated as a ready-made logo.
- No symbol is being invented just to fill a square.
- No candidate is being called “final” before it passes the gates and is approved.

## 11. Decision for the next phase

Proceed with a controlled lettering study informed by the three territories above. Produce a small number of genuinely different hand-authored constructions, not three decorative treatments of the same generic idea. Critique each against the gates before refining any into vector artwork.

The first deliverable after this research foundation should be a **lettering specimen sheet**: visible construction studies of “Vaani”, with annotations for stroke logic, spacing, counters, terminals, and the proposed signature gesture. It should include no invented cultural ornaments and no premature color palette.
